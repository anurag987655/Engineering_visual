## Why a field?

Coulomb's law tells us the size and direction of the force between two
charges. What it does not tell us is *how* that force crosses empty space.
Charge $Q_1$ pushes on $Q_2$ with nothing in between — no rod, no string,
no contact of any kind. This is the old "action at a distance" puzzle.

Field theory resolves it by splitting the interaction into two stages:

1. The **source charge** $Q$ modifies the space around it. Every point in
   that space acquires a new property — a direction and a strength — that
   was not there before. That modified space is the **electric field**.
2. Any **test charge** placed in the field interacts only with the field
   at its own location. The push it feels is local: the field right there
   does the work.

## Electric field

An **electric field** is a vector field that surrounds any charge
distribution. To every point in space we attach an arrow: the arrow's
direction is the direction a positive test charge would move, and the
arrow's length is proportional to how hard it would be pushed.

Two facts shape every field you will ever meet:

- The **direction** of the field depends on the sign of the source charge
  and on where you stand relative to it. Field arrows point away from a
  positive charge and toward a negative charge.
- The **magnitude** of the field follows an inverse-square law: double
  your distance from a point charge and the field drops to one quarter of
  its original strength.

<KeyIdea>
  The electric field belongs to the space itself. It is there whether or
  not a test charge is present to feel it — the test charge only reveals
  what the source charge has already set up.
</KeyIdea>

## Electric field intensity

The **electric field intensity** $\vec{E}$ is the quantitative version of
that idea: it is the force felt per unit positive test charge at a point.
Bring a small test charge $q_0$ to the point, measure the force $\vec{F}$
on it, and divide:

$$
\vec{E} = \lim_{q_0 \to 0} \frac{\vec{F}}{q_0}
$$


The limit is there for a reason. A test charge of its own produces a
field, and if it is large it can push the source charges around and
distort the very thing we are trying to measure. Letting $q_0$ shrink to
zero removes that disturbance, so $\vec{E}$ describes the field of the
source charges alone.

The units follow straight from the definition: newtons per coulomb
$(\mathrm{N/C})$, which is the same as volts per metre $(\mathrm{V/m})$.

**Step 1 — the displacement.** The directed segment from the source to
the field point is

$$
\vec{R} = \vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'
$$


with magnitude $R = |\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'|$ and unit vector

$$
\hat{a}_R = \frac{\vec{R}}{R} = \frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'|}
$$


**Step 2 — the force on a test charge.** Place a test charge $q_0$ at $P$.
Coulomb's law gives the force on it:

$$
\vec{F} = \frac{q_0 Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^2}\,\hat{a}_R
$$


**Step 3 — divide by the test charge.** By definition,
$\vec{E} = \vec{F}/q_0$, so the $q_0$ cancels and

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^2}\,\hat{a}_R
$$


**Step 4 — substitute the vectors.** Replacing $R$ and $\hat{a}_R$ with
their definitions gives the field in terms of the two position vectors:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{Q\,(\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,')}{4\pi\epsilon_0\,|\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'|^{3}}
$$


The power of three appears because one factor of $R$ comes from the
denominator $R^2$ and the other from normalising $\hat{a}_R$.

**Step 5 — Cartesian components.** Writing out
$\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,' = (x-x')\hat{a}_x + (y-y')\hat{a}_y + (z-z')\hat{a}_z$:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{Q\left[(x-x')\,\hat{a}_x + (y-y')\,\hat{a}_y + (z-z')\,\hat{a}_z\right]}{4\pi\epsilon_0\left[(x-x')^2 + (y-y')^2 + (z-z')^2\right]^{3/2}}
$$


**Step 6 — the special case of a charge at the origin.** If $Q$ sits at
the origin, $\vec{r}\,' = 0$ and $R = r$, the spherical radius. The field
becomes purely radial:

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^{2}}\,\hat{a}_r
$$


This is the inverse-square law we saw in the graph: the magnitude falls
as $1/r^2$, and for $Q > 0$ the field points outward along $\hat{a}_r$;
for $Q < 0$ it points inward.

appears — so each charge produces its field as if the others were not

By superposition, the total field is the vector sum:

$$
\boxed{\vec{E} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \frac{Q_k\,(\vec{r} - \vec{r}_k)}{4\pi\epsilon_0\,|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_k|^{3}}}
$$


<KeyIdea>
  Superposition: the field of several charges is the vector sum of the
  fields each would produce alone. There is no interaction term between
  source charges — only addition of arrows.
</KeyIdea>

## Electric field intensity due to a line charge

Charges are not always points. When charge is spread along a line we
describe it with a **line charge density**

$$
\rho_L = \frac{dQ}{dL} \qquad [\mathrm{C/m}]
$$


so that a length element carries $dQ = \rho_L\,dL$.

The assumptions for this derivation:

- an infinite straight line of uniform $\rho_L$ lies along the $z$-axis;
- the field point $P$ sits at perpendicular distance $\rho$ from the line;
- the geometry is **cylindrical**: nothing depends on $\phi$ or on $z$, so
  the field can only point radially, in the $\hat{a}_\rho$ direction.

**Step 1 — the source element.** Cut a length $dz$ from the line at
height $z$. It carries

$$
dQ = \rho_L\,dz
$$


**Step 2 — the field of that element.** By Coulomb's law the element
contributes

$$
d\vec{E} = \frac{\rho_L\,dz}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^{2}}\,\hat{a}_R
$$


where $R = \sqrt{z^2 + \rho^2}$ is the slant distance from the element to
$P$.

**Step 3 — kill the components that cancel.** For every element at $+z$
there is an identical element at $-z$. Their slant distances are equal,
so their $dE$ magnitudes are equal; by mirror symmetry the components
along the line cancel exactly, and only the components along $\rho$
survive. The surviving piece of one element is

$$
dE_\rho = dE\,\cos\theta = dE\cdot\frac{\rho}{R}
= \frac{\rho_L\,\rho\,dz}{4\pi\epsilon_0\left(z^2 + \rho^2\right)^{3/2}}
$$


**Step 4 — integrate over the whole line.** The surviving components all
point along $\hat{a}_\rho$, so we can add them as scalars over
$z \in (-\infty, \infty)$ — or, by symmetry, double the integral from
$0$ to $\infty$:

$$
E = \frac{\rho_L\,\rho}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\int_0^{\infty}
\frac{dz}{\left(z^2 + \rho^2\right)^{3/2}}
$$


**Step 5 — evaluate the integral.** The standard integral is

$$
\int \frac{dz}{\left(z^2 + \rho^2\right)^{3/2}}
= \frac{z}{\rho^2\sqrt{z^2 + \rho^2}}
$$


Evaluated from $0$ to $\infty$:

$$
\left[\frac{z}{\rho^2\sqrt{z^2 + \rho^2}}\right]_0^{\infty}
= \frac{1}{\rho^2} - 0 = \frac{1}{\rho^2}
$$


**Step 6 — assemble the result.**

$$
E = \frac{\rho_L\,\rho}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\cdot\frac{1}{\rho^2}
= \frac{\rho_L}{2\pi\epsilon_0 \rho}
$$


In cylindrical notation, with $\hat{a}_\rho$ pointing from the line out to
the field point:

$$
\boxed{\vec{E} = \frac{\rho_L}{2\pi\epsilon_0 \rho}\,\hat{a}_\rho}
$$


The field falls off as $1/\rho$, not $1/\rho^2$ — an infinite line keeps
sending field outward along its whole length, so the inverse-square law
softens. If the line does not lie on the $z$-axis, $\rho$ is still the
perpendicular distance from the line to the point, and $\hat{a}_\rho$
still points from the line toward the point.

## Electric field intensity due to an infinite sheet of charge

For charge spread over a surface we use the **surface charge density**

$$
\rho_S = \frac{dQ}{dS} \qquad [\mathrm{C/m^2}]
$$


Take an infinite sheet of uniform $\rho_S$ lying in the $yz$-plane
($x = 0$), and find the field at $P(x, 0, 0)$ on the $x$-axis.

Two symmetries fix the answer before any integration:

- Strips at $+y$ and $-y$ send equal fields whose $y$-components cancel,
  so only $E_x$ survives.
- The sheet is infinite in $y$ and $z$, so nothing depends on where you
  stand in the plane — the field can only depend on $x$.

$$
E = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}
\frac{\rho_S\,x\,dy}{2\pi\epsilon_0\left(x^2 + y^2\right)}
= \frac{\rho_S\,x}{2\pi\epsilon_0}
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dy}{x^2 + y^2}
$$


The remaining integral is standard:

$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{dy}{x^2 + y^2}
= \left[\frac{1}{x}\arctan\frac{y}{x}\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty}
= \frac{1}{x}\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right)
= \frac{\pi}{x}
$$


**Step 5 — assemble the result.**

$$
E = \frac{\rho_S\,x}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\cdot\frac{\pi}{x}
= \frac{\rho_S}{2\epsilon_0}
$$


The $x$ cancelled — the field of an infinite sheet does not depend on
distance at all. In general form, with $\hat{a}_N$ the unit normal
pointing away from the sheet:

$$
\boxed{\vec{E} = \frac{\rho_S}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{a}_N}
$$

**Step 1 — the two contributions.** From the previous section, the field
of an infinite sheet is $\rho_S / 2\epsilon_0$ pointing away from the
sheet. So:

- The $+\rho_S$ sheet at $x = 0$ pushes right for $x > 0$ and left for
  $x < 0$.
- The $-\rho_S$ sheet at $x = a$ pulls right for $x < a$ and left for
  $x > a$.

**Step 2 — add them in each region.**

1. **Region I, $x < 0$**
   - Positive sheet: $-\dfrac{\rho_S}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{a}_x$.
   - Negative sheet: $+\dfrac{\rho_S}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{a}_x$.
   - **Total field:** $\vec{E} = \vec{0}$.
2. **Region II, $0 < x < a$**
   - Positive sheet: $+\dfrac{\rho_S}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{a}_x$.
   - Negative sheet: $+\dfrac{\rho_S}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{a}_x$.
   - **Total field:** $\vec{E} = \dfrac{\rho_S}{\epsilon_0}\,\hat{a}_x$.
3. **Region III, $x > a$**
   - Positive sheet: $+\dfrac{\rho_S}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{a}_x$.
   - Negative sheet: $-\dfrac{\rho_S}{2\epsilon_0}\,\hat{a}_x$.
   - **Total field:** $\vec{E} = \vec{0}$.

**Step 3 — read the result.** Outside the plates the two fields cancel
exactly; between them they reinforce. The field of the capacitor is a
uniform field confined to the gap:

$$
\boxed{\vec{E} = \frac{\rho_S}{\epsilon_0}\,\hat{a}_x
\qquad (0 < x < a)}
$$


uniform in the gap and zero everywhere else — twice the field of a
single sheet, because both sheets contribute the same way inside.

## Streamlines

A **streamline** is a curve drawn through the field such that its
tangent at every point points in the direction of $\vec{E}$ there. Field
lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges; they never
cross, because a crossing would give two directions for the field at one
point.

**The streamline equation.** In two dimensions a streamline is a graph
$y(x)$, and its slope must match the slope of the field:

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{E_y}{E_x}
$$


That single relation is the equation of the streamlines: solve the
differential equation and every solution curve is a field line.

**Example — a point charge at the origin.** For $Q$ at the origin,

$$
\vec{E} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\left(x\,\hat{a}_x +
y\,\hat{a}_y\right)
$$


so the ratio of the components is

$$
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{E_y}{E_x}
= \frac{y}{x}
$$


Separate variables and integrate:

$$
\int \frac{dy}{y} = \int \frac{dx}{x}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\ln|y| = \ln|x| + \ln|C|
$$


$$
\boxed{y = Cx}
$$


Every streamline is a straight line through the charge — exactly the
radial pattern in the figure. The constant $C$ picks which line you are
on; the sign of $Q$ decides whether the arrows point out along those
lines or in toward the charge.
