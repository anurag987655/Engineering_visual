## From Electric Fields to Electric Flux

In the previous lesson, you saw that a charge changes the space around it. A positive source charge pushes a positive test charge away; a negative source charge pulls it inward. We described that influence with the **electric field intensity** $\vec{E}$: at every point, $\vec{E}$ tells you the direction and strength of the force that would act on a positive test charge placed there.

Now place an **imaginary surface** in that field. It could be a flat sheet of paper held in the air, a window frame, or a bubble drawn in space. The surface is not a physical barrier — it is a bookkeeping surface we choose so we can ask a simple question:

<KeyIdea>
  How much electric field passes through this surface?
</KeyIdea>

If the field is strong, or if the surface is large and faces the field directly, many field lines pierce it. If the surface is small, turned edge-on, or placed where the field is weak, few lines pierce it. **Electric flux** is the quantity that measures this “amount of field passing through.”

## Understanding Electric Flux (Ψ)

**Electric flux** is the total electric displacement flux passing through a surface. We denote it by the Greek letter $\Psi$ (Psi).

- $\Psi$ — electric flux (a total amount over a surface).

The word *total* matters. Flux is not defined at a single point the way $\vec{E}$ or $\vec{D}$ is. Instead, it adds up the contribution from the whole surface.

### Physical interpretation

Imagine electric displacement as something that “flows” through the area you chose. Where field lines cross the surface densely and head-on, the contribution is large. Where they skim along the surface, the contribution is zero. Flux counts the net crossing over the entire surface.

### A scalar quantity

Electric flux $\Psi$ is a **scalar**. It has magnitude and sign (positive, negative, or zero, depending on direction relative to a chosen normal), but it does not have a direction like a vector. This is different from electric flux density $\vec{D}$, which is a vector field defined at each point.

### Relationship with surface area and field strength

For the same field, a larger surface can intercept more field. For the same surface, a stronger field produces more flux. Orientation also matters — we will make that precise when we introduce the surface normal and the dot product.

### Units

In SI units, electric flux $\Psi$ is measured in **coulombs (C)**. You will see this is consistent once we define electric flux density in $\text{C/m}^2$ and multiply by area in $\text{m}^2$.

<Callout title="Why coulombs?" variant="note">
  Electric flux is often called <em>electric displacement flux</em>. In this course’s convention, its total amount through a surface is expressed in coulombs, matching the units you obtain from $\vec{D}$ integrated over area.
</Callout>

## Electric Flux Density (D)

To describe how concentrated the flux is at a surface, we use **electric flux density** $\vec{D}$.

For a **uniform** field that is **perpendicular** to a **flat** surface, the density is the flux per unit area:

$$
D = \frac{\Psi}{S}
$$

- $D$ — magnitude of electric flux density (for this special uniform, perpendicular case).
- $\Psi$ — total electric flux through the surface, in coulombs (C).
- $S$ — surface area, in square metres ($\text{m}^2$).

Rearranging gives the equivalent uniform, perpendicular form $\Psi = D S$.

### Physical meaning

$\vec{D}$ tells you how much electric flux passes through each unit of area when you look locally at the field. A larger $\vec{D}$ means more flux packed into each square metre (for a surface facing the field).

### Vector nature and units

Electric flux density $\vec{D}$ is a **vector**. Its direction is the direction of electric displacement at that point, and its magnitude tells you the flux per unit area perpendicular to that direction. Its SI unit is **coulombs per square metre ($\text{C/m}^2$)**.

### Flux versus flux density

| Quantity | Symbol | Type | Unit | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Electric flux | $\Psi$ | Scalar | C | Total displacement flux through a surface |
| Electric flux density | $\vec{D}$ | Vector | $\text{C/m}^2$ | Flux per unit area (locally directed) |

### Different areas, same perpendicular field

If the same uniform $\vec{D}$ passes perpendicularly through two surfaces, the larger surface intercepts more total flux even though the density is the same.

## Relationship Between Electric Field Intensity and Flux Density

Electric field intensity $\vec{E}$ and electric flux density $\vec{D}$ are closely related, but they are not the same quantity. The material occupying the space affects how $\vec{D}$ responds to $\vec{E}$.

### Free space

In free space (vacuum), the relationship is

$$
\vec{D} = \varepsilon_0 \vec{E}
$$

- $\varepsilon_0$ — **permittivity of free space**, a constant that sets how electric displacement relates to the electric field in vacuum. Its value is approximately $8.854 \times 10^{-12}\,\text{F/m}$.

### Linear isotropic dielectric

In a simple (linear, isotropic) dielectric material, the material responds proportionally and in the same direction as the applied field:

$$
\vec{D} = \varepsilon \vec{E} = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r \vec{E}
$$

- $\varepsilon$ — **permittivity** of the material ($\text{F/m}$).
- $\varepsilon_r$ — **relative permittivity** (dielectric constant), a dimensionless number comparing the material’s permittivity to $\varepsilon_0$.

### General macroscopic relationship

More generally, the macroscopic constitutive relationship can be written as

$$
\vec{D} = \varepsilon_0 \vec{E} + \vec{P}
$$

- $\vec{P}$ — **polarization**, the electric dipole moment per unit volume produced when the material’s bound charges shift slightly in response to the field.

### Intuition: why the material matters

An applied electric field $\vec{E}$ exerts forces on charges inside matter. Bound charges can shift a little, creating polarization $\vec{P}$. That internal response contributes to the overall electric displacement $\vec{D}$. In vacuum there is no material to polarize, so $\vec{P} = \vec{0}$ and we recover $\vec{D} = \varepsilon_0 \vec{E}$. In a linear isotropic dielectric, this response can be summarized by $\varepsilon_r$.

<Callout title="Keep E and D distinct" variant="note">
  $\vec{E}$ describes the electric field intensity (force per unit charge). $\vec{D}$ describes electric flux density (displacement flux per unit area). They point in the same direction in linear isotropic media, but they are different physical quantities with different units.
</Callout>

## Area as a Vector

So far, area sounded like “how big the surface is.” But to compute flux correctly, **orientation** matters just as much as size. Turning a surface can change the flux even when its area stays the same. To capture both magnitude and orientation, we treat a small surface element as a vector.

For a differential surface element of magnitude $dS$, define

$$
d\vec{S} = \hat{a}_n\, dS
$$

- $d\vec{S}$ — **vector area element** ($\text{m}^2$ in magnitude), pointing normal to the surface.
- $dS$ — **scalar area** (magnitude) of the small element.
- $\hat{a}_n$ — **unit normal vector** (dimensionless, magnitude 1) perpendicular to the surface element.

### Direction and choice of normal

At any smooth surface point there are two opposite perpendicular directions. You must **choose** which one is the positive normal direction and use it consistently:

- For an **open surface**, the choice is set by convention or by the problem (often indicated by an arrow).
- For a **closed surface**, the positive normal is usually taken as **outward**.

Once $\hat{a}_n$ is chosen, the sign of flux through that element follows from how $\vec{D}$ aligns with $\hat{a}_n$.

## Surface Orientation and Electric Flux

Let $\theta$ be the angle between the electric flux density $\vec{D}$ and the **surface normal** $\hat{a}_n$ (not the angle between $\vec{D}$ and the plane of the surface).

For a **uniform** $\vec{D}$ over a **flat** surface of area $S$ with constant orientation, the flux is

$$
\Psi = D S \cos\theta
$$

- $D$ — magnitude of $\vec{D}$ ($\text{C/m}^2$).
- $S$ — area of the flat surface ($\text{m}^2$).
- $\theta$ — angle between $\vec{D}$ and the chosen normal $\hat{a}_n$ (radians or degrees).
- $\cos\theta$ — picks out how much of $\vec{D}$ passes through the surface along the normal.

This comes from the dot product idea: only the component of $\vec{D}$ along $\hat{a}_n$ contributes to crossing the surface. The effective “projected” coupling is $S\cos\theta$ relative to the field direction.

### Three important cases

- **$\theta = 0^\circ$ (maximum positive flux):** $\vec{D}$ is aligned with the chosen normal, $\cos 0^\circ = 1$, so $\Psi = DS$.
- **$\theta = 90^\circ$ (zero flux):** $\vec{D}$ is parallel to the surface and perpendicular to the normal, $\cos 90^\circ = 0$, so $\Psi = 0$.
- **$\theta = 180^\circ$ (maximum negative flux):** $\vec{D}$ is opposite to the chosen normal, $\cos 180^\circ = -1$, so $\Psi = -DS$.

The sign tells you whether the net displacement flux crosses the surface in the **same** sense as the chosen normal (positive) or the **opposite** sense (negative).

## Surface Integration

The formula $\Psi = D S \cos\theta$ works when $\vec{D}$ is uniform, the surface is flat, and $\theta$ is the same everywhere on it. Real situations are often more complicated: the field can vary from point to point, the surface can be curved, or the angle between $\vec{D}$ and the local normal can change across the surface.

### A small element

Divide the surface into many tiny elements. Over a sufficiently small element, $\vec{D}$ and the orientation are approximately constant. The flux through one differential element is

$$
d\Psi = \vec{D} \cdot d\vec{S}
$$

- $d\Psi$ — differential (small) contribution to the total flux.
- $\vec{D}$ — electric flux density at the element.
- $d\vec{S} = \hat{a}_n dS$ — vector area element at the same location.

### Adding all contributions

Total flux is the sum of all these small contributions over the entire surface $S$:

$$
\Psi = \int_S \vec{D} \cdot d\vec{S}
$$

- $\int_S (\cdot)$ — surface integral over the chosen surface $S$.

Geometrically, the dot product keeps only the normal component at each element: $d\Psi = D\, dS \cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the local angle between $\vec{D}$ and $\hat{a}_n$. Adding (integrating) these pieces accumulates the net flux.

### Connection to the simple formula

If $\vec{D}$ has constant magnitude and direction, the surface is flat, and $\hat{a}_n$ is constant so that $\theta$ is the same everywhere, then $\vec{D}\cdot \hat{a}_n = D\cos\theta$ can be taken outside the integral and $\int_S dS = S$, giving $\Psi = D S \cos\theta$ as before.

## Open and Closed Surfaces

A surface used for flux calculations can be **open** or **closed**.

- **Open surface:** has an edge or boundary (like a sheet, a disk, or a looped frame). Field lines can pass through it, and you choose a positive normal direction.
- **Closed surface:** completely encloses a volume (like a sphere or a box). It has no boundary edge. By convention, the positive normal $\hat{a}_n$ points **outward** everywhere.

For a closed surface, we often write the integral with a circle on the integral sign:

$$
\Psi = \oint_S \vec{D} \cdot d\vec{S}
$$

- $\oint_S$ — integral over a closed surface $S$.

If a positive charge sits inside a closed surface, its outward field lines cross the surface from inside to outside, contributing net outward flux. In this article we focus on seeing and computing that flux; we will not turn this into a law relating enclosed charge and flux.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Uniform perpendicular field

**Given:** A uniform electric displacement field of magnitude $D = 5\,\mu\text{C/m}^2$ passes perpendicularly through a flat surface of area $S = 2\,\text{m}^2$.

**Required:** Electric flux $\Psi$.

**Relevant formula:** For a uniform field perpendicular to a flat surface, $\theta = 0^\circ$ and

$$
\Psi = D S
$$

**Substitution:**

$$
\Psi = \left(5\,\mu\text{C/m}^2\right)\left(2\,\text{m}^2\right)
$$

**Calculation:**

$$
\Psi = 10\,\mu\text{C}
$$

**Final answer:** $\Psi = 10\,\mu\text{C}$.

### Example 2: Field at an angle to the normal

**Given:** A uniform electric displacement field of magnitude $D = 8\,\mu\text{C/m}^2$ passes through a flat surface of area $S = 0.5\,\text{m}^2$. The angle between the field and the **surface normal** is $\theta = 60^\circ$.

**Required:** Electric flux $\Psi$.

**Relevant formula:** For uniform $\vec{D}$ on a flat surface,

$$
\Psi = D S \cos\theta
$$

**Substitution:**

$$
\Psi = \left(8\,\mu\text{C/m}^2\right)\left(0.5\,\text{m}^2\right)\cos 60^\circ
$$

**Calculation:** $\cos 60^\circ = 0.5$, so

$$
\Psi = (8)(0.5)(0.5)\,\mu\text{C} = 2\,\mu\text{C}
$$

**Final answer:** $\Psi = 2\,\mu\text{C}$.

### Example 3: Finding flux density

**Given:** A flat surface of area $S = 0.25\,\text{m}^2$ experiences an electric flux $\Psi = 4\,\mu\text{C}$. The field is perpendicular to the surface and uniform.

**Required:** Electric flux density magnitude $D$.

**Relevant formula:** For a uniform field perpendicular to a flat surface,

$$
D = \frac{\Psi}{S}
$$

**Substitution:**

$$
D = \frac{4\,\mu\text{C}}{0.25\,\text{m}^2}
$$

**Calculation:**

$$
D = 16\,\mu\text{C/m}^2
$$

**Final answer:** $D = 16\,\mu\text{C/m}^2$ (in the direction of $\vec{D}$, normal to the surface).

  }
