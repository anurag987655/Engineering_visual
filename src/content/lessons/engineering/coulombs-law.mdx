## Two charges in free space

Take two positive charges in free space and leave them alone for a
moment. Now push them closer together. Look at what happens to the
force between them: it pushes back harder the closer you try to bring
them. Pull the charges apart instead, and the very same force grows
weaker — far enough away, it vanishes completely.

The same story plays out with two negative charges. Negatives also push
each other away, and that push behaves with distance in exactly the
same way.

So what happens if the charges are not alike? Take one positive charge
and one negative charge. Now the force does the opposite job: it pulls
the charges together, and it pulls harder the closer they get.

## Coulomb's law

Every behaviour we just watched is captured by one rule, published by
Charles-Augustin de Coulomb in 1785 after a careful set of experiments
with charged spheres.

<KeyIdea>
  The force between two point charges acts along the straight line that
  joins them. Its magnitude grows with the product of the two charges
  and shrinks with the square of the distance between them. Charges of
  the same sign repel each other; charges of opposite signs attract
  each other.
</KeyIdea>

## Magnitude of the force

Start with what we saw in the scene. Bigger charges meant a bigger
force, so the force must grow with each charge:

$$F \propto Q_1 Q_2$$

Double either charge and the force doubles. Double both, and it grows
four times over.

We also saw the force die off with distance. Careful measurement shows
that it is not just any drop — it is an inverse-square drop:

$$F \propto \frac{1}{R^2}$$

Double the distance and the force falls to one quarter. Triple it, and
the force falls to one ninth.

Put the two proportionalities together, and bring in one constant that
describes how strongly free space pushes back:

$$F = K\,\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{R^2} \;=\; \frac{Q_1 Q_2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^2}$$

where:

- $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are the quantities of electric charge, measured in
  coulombs (C),
- $R$ is the distance between the two point charges, measured in metres
  (m),
- $K$ is the proportionality constant for free space (a vacuum):

$$K = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \approx 8.988 \times 10^{9}\;\left[\mathrm{N\cdot m^{2}/C^{2}}\right]$$

- $\epsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space, which measures how
  easily an electric field forms in a vacuum:

$$\epsilon_0 \approx 8.854 \times 10^{-12}\;\left[\mathrm{F/m}\right]$$

<Callout title="A quick feel for the numbers" variant="note">
  Two charges of $1\,\mu\text{C}$ sitting $2\text{ m}$ apart feel a force
  of about $2.25\text{ mN}$ — roughly the weight of two or three paper
  clips. Slide them to $1\text{ m}$ apart and the force climbs to
  $8.99\text{ mN}$: four times as strong, from half the distance.
</Callout>

## Direction of the force

Magnitude tells us how strong the force is. We still need to pin down
which way it points. The playground above already draws both arrows —
$\vec{F}_{12}$ and $\vec{F}_{21}$ — for like charges and for opposite
charges. Now let us derive them properly.

First, a naming convention. $\vec{F}_{12}$ means "the force on charge 2
caused by charge 1". The first subscript is the charge that feels the
force; the second is the charge that causes it.

Put $Q_1$ at position $\vec{r}_1$ and $Q_2$ at position $\vec{r}_2$. The
displacement vector pointing from $Q_1$ to $Q_2$ is

$$\vec{R}_{12} = \vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1$$

To talk about direction alone, we strip off the length and keep a unit
vector — a little arrow of length one that points along $\vec{R}_{12}$:

$$\hat{a}_{R12} = \frac{\vec{R}_{12}}{|\vec{R}_{12}|} = \frac{\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|}$$

The force on $Q_2$ due to $Q_1$ is then

$$\vec{F}_{12} = \frac{Q_1 Q_2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R_{12}^{2}}\,\hat{a}_{R12} = \frac{Q_1 Q_2 (\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1)}{4\pi\epsilon_0\,|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|^{3}}$$

The sign of the product $Q_1 Q_2$ decides everything:

- **Like charges** ($Q_1 Q_2 > 0$): the product is positive, so
  $\vec{F}_{12}$ points in the same direction as $\vec{R}_{12}$ — away
  from $Q_1$. That is repulsion.
- **Opposite charges** ($Q_1 Q_2 < 0$): the product is negative, so
  $\vec{F}_{12}$ points opposite to $\vec{R}_{12}$ — back toward $Q_1$.
  That is attraction.

And what about the force on $Q_1$? It has the same size and points
exactly the opposite way:

$$\vec{F}_{21} = -\vec{F}_{12}$$

This is Newton's third law at work, and you can watch it happen in the
playground: the two arrows always have the same length, and they always
point opposite ways.

## Principle of superposition

One pair of charges is manageable. What about a whole crowd? Coulomb's
law is linear — no products of different source charges appear in it —
so each charge behaves as though the others were not there at all. To
find the total force on a charge, apply Coulomb's law to each pair
separately, then add the resulting arrows tip to tip.

When $n$ point charges $Q_1, Q_2, \dots, Q_n$ act at the same time on a
test charge $Q_t$ located at position $\vec{r}$, the net force is simply
the vector sum of the individual forces:

$$\vec{F}_{\text{total}} = \vec{F}_{1t} + \vec{F}_{2t} + \dots + \vec{F}_{nt} = \sum_{m=1}^{n} \frac{Q_m Q_t (\vec{r} - \vec{r}_m)}{4\pi\epsilon_0\,|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_m|^{3}}$$

Each term in that sum is just Coulomb's law in vector form, written for
one source charge at a time. Compute each arrow, then add them tip to
tip — that is the whole principle.
