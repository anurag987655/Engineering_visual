## Rectangular coordinate system

A rectangular coordinate system, also commonly known as the Cartesian
coordinate system, is defined by three coordinates ($x$, $y$ and $z$). It
represents the intersection of three mutually perpendicular planes:

- $x$ is a constant plane that is parallel to the $yz$ plane,
- $y$ is a constant plane that is parallel to the $xz$ plane,
- $z$ is a constant plane that is parallel to the $xy$ plane.

The coordinates can take any value in the range

$$
-\infty < x < \infty, \qquad
-\infty < y < \infty, \qquad
-\infty < z < \infty
$$

The axes follow a right-handed coordinate system, meaning that if the
fingers of the right hand curl from the positive $x$-axis toward the
positive $y$-axis, the thumb points along the positive $z$-axis.

<Callout title="Right-hand rule" variant="note">
  Point the fingers of your right hand along $+x$, curl them toward $+y$ —
  your thumb points along $+z$. Flip any one axis and the system becomes
  left-handed, and every cross product in it changes sign.
</Callout>

## Vector representation and unit vectors

Any vector in rectangular coordinates is written in terms of its
orthogonal components $A_x$, $A_y$ and $A_z$:

$$
\vec{A} = A_x\,\hat{a}_x + A_y\,\hat{a}_y + A_z\,\hat{a}_z
$$

or, stacked vertically as a column,

$$
\vec{A} =
\begin{bmatrix}
A_x \\
A_y \\
A_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

where $\hat{a}_x$, $\hat{a}_y$ and $\hat{a}_z$ are constant everywhere in
space and represent the unit vectors that point along the direction of
increasing $x$, $y$ and $z$ respectively.

The magnitude of the vector $\vec{A}$ is given by

$$
|\vec{A}| = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2}
$$

## Cylindrical coordinate system

The three variables used in cylindrical coordinates are:

- $\rho$: radial distance in the $xy$ plane ($0$ to $\infty$),
- $\phi$: angle made with respect to the positive $x$ axis
  ($0$ to $2\pi$),
- $z$: height along the $z$ axis ($-\infty$ to $\infty$).

<Callout title="A note on symbols" variant="note">
  This lesson writes cylindrical coordinates as $(\rho, \phi, z)$ and
  spherical coordinates as $(r, \theta, \phi)$ — the same notation the
  past-year questions at the end of this chapter use. $\phi$ is the azimuth
  in **both** systems; $\rho$ is the cylindrical radius, $r$ the spherical
  radius, and $\theta$ the polar angle from $+z$ (spherical only).
</Callout>

## Rectangular and cylindrical conversions

### Rectangular $(x, y, z)$ to cylindrical $(\rho, \phi, z)$

Given a point $P(x, y, z)$ in Cartesian coordinates:

1. Radial distance:

   $$
   \rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}
   $$

2. Azimuthal angle:

   $$
   \phi = \arctan\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)
   $$

   **Note:** inspect the signs of $x$ and $y$ to place $\phi$ in the
   correct quadrant — in practice this is `atan2(y, x)`, which already
   resolves the quadrant for you.

3. Height:

   $$
   z = z
   $$

### Cylindrical $(\rho, \phi, z)$ to rectangular $(x, y, z)$

Given a point $P(\rho, \phi, z)$ in cylindrical coordinates:

1. $x$-coordinate:

   $$
   x = \rho \cos\phi
   $$

2. $y$-coordinate:

   $$
   y = \rho \sin\phi
   $$

3. $z$-coordinate:

   $$
   z = z
   $$

## Direction of $\hat{a}_\rho$, $\hat{a}_\phi$ and $\hat{a}_z$

Toggle the unit vectors in the playground above to see them at the point
you chose. At any point, $\hat{a}_\rho$ points straight outward from the
$z$-axis, $\hat{a}_\phi$ is perpendicular to it in the direction of
increasing angle, and $\hat{a}_z$ points straight up. The three are
mutually perpendicular, and — unlike $\hat{a}_x$, $\hat{a}_y$, $\hat{a}_z$ —
they **change direction as you move around the space**.

## Establishing the transformation matrix

### 1. Cartesian to cylindrical components

To find a component, project the vector onto the direction we want (the
playground below does exactly this, in three dimensions — the vector sits
off the floor plane whenever $A_z \neq 0$, and you can orbit the scene to
see it). The radial direction makes an angle $\phi$ with the $x$-axis, so
the $x$-part contributes $A_x\cos\phi$ to the radial component. The
$y$-part contributes $A_y\sin\phi$. Adding these gives:

$$
A_\rho = A_x\cos\phi + A_y\sin\phi
$$

The tangential direction is perpendicular to the radial direction. Its
$x$-direction projection is $-\sin\phi$, while its $y$-direction
projection is $\cos\phi$. Therefore:

$$
A_\phi = -A_x\sin\phi + A_y\cos\phi
$$

The $z$-direction is the same in both coordinate systems, so:

$$
A_z = A_z
$$

Slide $A_z$ in the playground below and the teal arrow moves along the
$z$-axis while the two in-plane projections stay exactly where they were —
the third row of the matrix is there to leave that component alone.

Together, these equations can be written as:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A_\rho \\
A_\phi \\
A_z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\phi & \sin\phi & 0 \\
-\sin\phi & \cos\phi & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
}_{M}
\begin{bmatrix}
A_x \\
A_y \\
A_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

<Playground id="vector-projection" />

### 2. Use the orthogonal matrix property to convert back

The transformation matrix $M$ is an orthogonal matrix. For an orthogonal
matrix, the inverse is equal to its transpose:

$$
M^{-1} = M^{T}
$$

The transpose is obtained by swapping rows and columns. Therefore, to
convert from cylindrical components back to Cartesian components, multiply
by $M^{T}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A_x \\
A_y \\
A_z
\end{bmatrix}
= M^{T}
\begin{bmatrix}
A_\rho \\
A_\phi \\
A_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

So the inverse transformation is:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A_x \\
A_y \\
A_z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\phi & -\sin\phi & 0 \\
\sin\phi & \cos\phi & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
A_\rho \\
A_\phi \\
A_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Which gives:

$$
A_x = A_\rho\cos\phi - A_\phi\sin\phi
$$

$$
A_y = A_\rho\sin\phi + A_\phi\cos\phi
$$

$$
A_z = A_z
$$

<KeyIdea>
  The matrix is a rotation about the $z$-axis. Rotating there never changes
  a vector's length, and undoing a rotation is just turning back — which is
  why $M^{-1} = M^{T}$, and why the round trip returns exactly the
  components you started with.
</KeyIdea>

## Spherical coordinate system

The three variables used in spherical coordinates are:

- $r$: radial distance from the origin ($0$ to $\infty$),
- $\theta$: polar angle measured from the positive $z$ axis
  ($0$ to $\pi$),
- $\phi$: azimuthal angle measured from the positive $x$ axis
  ($0$ to $2\pi$).

### Rectangular $(x, y, z)$ to spherical $(r, \theta, \phi)$

Given a point $P(x, y, z)$ in Cartesian coordinates:

1. Radial distance:

   $$
   r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}
   $$

2. Polar angle:

   $$
   \theta = \arccos\left(\frac{z}{r}\right)
   $$

   **Note:** because $r \geq 0$, this already returns a value in
   $[0, \pi]$ — no quadrant hunting needed.

3. Azimuthal angle:

   $$
   \phi = \arctan\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)
   $$

   **Note:** inspect the signs of $x$ and $y$ to place $\phi$ in the
   correct quadrant, i.e. use `atan2(y, x)`.

### Spherical $(r, \theta, \phi)$ to rectangular $(x, y, z)$

Given a point $P(r, \theta, \phi)$ in spherical coordinates:

1. $x$-coordinate:

   $$
   x = r \sin\theta \cos\phi
   $$

2. $y$-coordinate:

   $$
   y = r \sin\theta \sin\phi
   $$

3. $z$-coordinate:

   $$
   z = r \cos\theta
   $$

## Spherical transformation matrix

The same projection argument as before, run on the two new directions.
Projecting $\vec{A}$ onto $\hat{a}_r$, $\hat{a}_\theta$ and $\hat{a}_\phi$
one component at a time gives:

$$
A_r = A_x\sin\theta\cos\phi + A_y\sin\theta\sin\phi + A_z\cos\theta
$$

$$
A_\theta = A_x\cos\theta\cos\phi + A_y\cos\theta\sin\phi - A_z\sin\theta
$$

$$
A_\phi = -A_x\sin\phi + A_y\cos\phi
$$

Together:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A_r \\
A_\theta \\
A_\phi
\end{bmatrix}
=
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\sin\theta\cos\phi & \sin\theta\sin\phi & \cos\theta \\
\cos\theta\cos\phi & \cos\theta\sin\phi & -\sin\theta \\
-\sin\phi & \cos\phi & 0
\end{bmatrix}
}_{M}
\begin{bmatrix}
A_x \\
A_y \\
A_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$M$ is orthogonal again, so the way back is the transpose — swap rows for
columns and you have the inverse:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A_x \\
A_y \\
A_z
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\sin\theta\cos\phi & \cos\theta\cos\phi & -\sin\phi \\
\sin\theta\sin\phi & \cos\theta\sin\phi & \cos\phi \\
\cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
A_r \\
A_\theta \\
A_\phi
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The pattern is identical in all three systems: **write the direction you
want in terms of the basis you have, read off the projections, stack them
into a matrix, and invert by transposing.**
