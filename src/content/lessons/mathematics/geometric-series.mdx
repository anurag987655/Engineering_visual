## Definition

A geometric series is an infinite sum pinned down by two values:

- $a$: the first term of the series
- $\rho$: the common ratio — the constant factor between consecutive terms

It is written as

$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} a\rho^{n-1}
$$

Reading off the terms: at $n = 1$ the exponent is zero, so the first term
is $a$. At $n = 2$ you get $a\rho$, then $a\rho^2$, and so on. Each
step multiplies the previous term by the same ratio.

## Sum of the Series

The sum is found by evaluating the partial sum and then taking its limit.

Let

$$
S_n = a + a\rho + a\rho^2 + \cdots + a\rho^{n-1}
$$

Multiply both sides by $\rho$, which shifts every term one step closer to
the tail:

$$
\rho S_n = a\rho + a\rho^2 + \cdots + a\rho^n
$$

Subtract the second equation from the first. Almost everything cancels:

$$
S_n - \rho S_n = a - a\rho^n
$$

$$
S_n(1-\rho) = a(1-\rho^n)
$$

So, for $\rho \neq 1$,

$$
S_n = \frac{a(1-\rho^n)}{1-\rho}
$$

The infinite sum is the limit of the partial sums:

$$
S = \lim_{n\to\infty} S_n
$$

If $|\rho| < 1$, then $\rho^n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$, which leaves

$$
S = \frac{a}{1-\rho}
$$

> **Why the sum makes sense.** The telescoping cancellation above is the
> same trick used throughout engineering: shift a weighted copy of a sum and
> subtract. It reappears in tail bounds, rolling averages, and discounted
> cash flows.

## Convergence

The series converges to a finite sum exactly when each new term shrinks
fast enough for the accumulation to settle:

$$
|\rho| < 1
$$

because then $\rho^n \to 0$ and the limit above exists, giving

$$
S = \frac{a}{1-\rho}
$$

If $|\rho| \ge 1$, the series does not converge to a finite sum. The full
picture, at a glance:

| Common ratio         | Behaviour                                           |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| $|\rho| < 1$         | converges to $\frac{a}{1-\rho}$                     |
| $\rho > 1$           | grows without bound                                 |
| $\rho = 1$           | terms never go to zero — grows linearly             |
| $\rho = -1$          | oscillates between $a$ and $0$ forever              |
| $\rho < -1$          | oscillates with growing magnitude                  |

The borderline case $|\rho| = 1$ needs direct checking, because the terms
never shrink toward zero.

## The Ratio Test

The ratio test decides convergence from the ratio of two consecutive terms.
Let

$$
L = \lim_{n\to\infty} \left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|
$$

Then:

- If $L < 1$, the series converges.
- If $L > 1$, the series diverges.
- If $L = 1$, the test is inconclusive.

For a geometric series, $a_n = a\rho^{n-1}$, so the ratio of consecutive
terms is exactly

$$
L = |\rho|
$$

The test therefore recovers the same condition: converge when $|\rho| < 1$,
diverge when $|\rho| > 1$, and check the boundary $|\rho| = 1$ directly.

## Applications

The geometric series is the baseline tool for **time value of money**
problems. Money today is worth more than the same money received later, so
future payments are discounted by the factor

$$
\frac{1}{1+r}
$$

where $r > 0$ is the interest rate.

### Present value of a perpetuity

An infinite stream of constant payments $V$, one at the end of each period,
forms a geometric series:

$$
PV = \frac{V}{1+r} + \frac{V}{(1+r)^2} + \frac{V}{(1+r)^3} + \cdots
$$

$$
PV = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{V}{1+r}\left(\frac{1}{1+r}\right)^{t-1}
$$

The first term is $a = \frac{V}{1+r}$ and the common ratio is
$\rho = \frac{1}{1+r} < 1$. Since $S = \frac{a}{1-\rho}$,

$$
PV = \frac{\frac{V}{1+r}}{1-\frac{1}{1+r}}
$$

$$
PV = \frac{V}{r}
$$

A forever-stream of $V$ is worth $V/r$ today.

> **Worked example.** A commercial project pays USD 500,000 per year
> forever, starting at $t = 1$, discounted at $r = 0.08$. Its present value
> is
>
> $$
> PV = \frac{500{,}000}{0.08} = 6{,}250{,}000
> $$
>
> If the project costs USD 10,000,000 at $t = 0$, the net present value is
>
> $$
> NPV = 6{,}250{,}000 - 10{,}000{,}000 = -3{,}750{,}000
> $$
>
> Since $NPV < 0$, the project is **not profitable**.

### The Keynesian expenditure multiplier

When an initial expenditure $I$ enters an economy, recipients save part and
spend the rest. The fraction they spend is the marginal propensity to
consume, $c$, with $0 < c < 1$. Spending recycles in a diminishing cycle of
rounds:

$$
\Delta Y = I + cI + c^2I + c^3I + \cdots
$$

$$
\Delta Y = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} I c^{t-1}
$$

Because $|c| < 1$, the series converges:

$$
\Delta Y = \frac{I}{1-c}
$$

> **Key idea.** Both formulas — perpetuity and multiplier — are the same
> geometric sum $\frac{a}{1-\rho}$ wearing different clothes. Recognize the
> shape, and the answer is one division. For an example: a stimulus of
> USD 20 billion with $c = 0.7$ yields
> $\Delta Y = \frac{20}{1-0.7} = \frac{20}{0.3} \approx 66.67$ billion.

### The growing perpetuity

Suppose a dividend grows at a constant rate $g$, so

$$
C_t = C(1+g)^{t-1}
$$

discounted at rate $r$. The present value is

$$
PV = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{C(1+g)^{t-1}}{(1+r)^t}
$$

Rewrite it as a geometric series:

$$
PV = \frac{C}{1+r} \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^{t-1}
$$

Now $a = \frac{C}{1+r}$ and $\rho = \frac{1+g}{1+r}$. Convergence needs
$|\rho| < 1$, which for positive rates means

$$
r > g
$$

Applying $S = \frac{a}{1-\rho}$:

$$
PV = \frac{\frac{C}{1+r}}{1 - \frac{1+g}{1+r}}
$$

$$
PV = \frac{C}{r-g}, \quad r > g
$$
