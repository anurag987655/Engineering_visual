## Why approximate?

A calculator does not *know* $\sin(37°)$. It does not have a table of every
angle either — your phone evaluates $\sin$, $\cos$, $e^x$ and $\ln$ the same
way it evaluates $3x^2 + 1$: with multiplication and addition. Behind nearly
every function a computer draws, solves, or simulates sits a **polynomial**.

So the question becomes: given a function $f$ and one point $x_0$ where you
*do* know everything about it — its value, its slope, how the slope bends, how
the bend twists — how much of the whole function can you reconstruct from that
one point?

Taylor's answer: all of it, if you keep going forever. And a sharply
quantified amount of it, if you stop early.

<Callout title="Worth knowing" variant="note">
  This is not just engineering convenience. The same infinite polynomial, plus
  the error bound you will meet below, is the classic proof that{" "}
  <strong>$e$ is irrational</strong> — a fact about numbers, smuggled out of an
  approximation tool.
</Callout>

## Linear approximation

Start with the least you can match at $x_0$: the **value** and the **slope**.
A straight line with those two properties is the tangent line:

$$
f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0)
$$

This is legitimate because it is exactly what the derivative says. Rearranging
the definition $f'(x_0) = \lim_{h\to 0} \frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}$, the
difference between the function and the line is small *faster than* $h$ itself
as $h \to 0$.

A quick use: to estimate $e^{0.1}$, take $x_0 = 0$, where $f(0) = 1$ and
$f'(0) = 1$:

$$
e^{0.1} \approx 1 + 1 \cdot (0.1) = 1.1
$$

The true value is $1.10517\ldots$ — the line is already right to one decimal.
Move to $x = 1$ with the same line and you get $2$ instead of $2.718$: the
tangent only speaks for its own neighborhood.

## Quadratic approximation

The line matches the slope but forces the curvature to zero. Let the
approximation **bend** by matching the second derivative as well:

$$
f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \frac{f''(x_0)}{2!}(x - x_0)^2
$$

Why exactly $\frac{f''(x_0)}{2}$? The slope does not stay constant — it
changes at rate $f''(x_0)$ across the gap, so it gains $f''(x_0)(x-x_0)$
along the way. Averaging the starting slope with the gained slope gives half
of it, which integrates into the $\tfrac{1}{2}f''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2$ term. The
factorial is already here, and it will keep growing.

By now the pattern has outgrown any single formula. Each step multiplies by
$\frac{(x - x_0)}{\text{one more integer}}$:

$$
e^{0.1} \approx 1 \;\to\; 1.1 \;\to\; 1.105 \;\to\; 1.1051667 \;\to\; \ldots
$$

True value: $1.1051709\ldots$

<KeyIdea>
  Every extra term buys one more matched derivative at $x_0$. The polynomial
  $P_n$ and the function $f$ do not just cross at $x_0$ — they share value,
  slope, curvature, and every twist up to order $n$. That is why the fit keeps
  improving <em>around</em> $x_0$, and why it can still fail far away.
</KeyIdea>

## The Taylor series

Read the three approximations as one recipe. The degree-$n$ Taylor polynomial
of $f$ centered at $x_0$ is

$$
P_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x - x_0)^k
$$

with the understanding that $f^{(0)} = f$ and $0! = 1$, so the $k = 0$ term is
just the base value $f(x_0)$. Expand the sum and the earlier formulas fall out
term by term:

$$
P_n(x) = f(x_0) + \frac{f'(x_0)}{1!}(x - x_0) + \frac{f''(x_0)}{2!}(x - x_0)^2 + \dots + \frac{f^{(n)}(x_0)}{n!}(x - x_0)^n
$$

Now do the thing that makes it a *series* instead of a polynomial: let
$n \to \infty$. If the partial sums settle on a limit, that infinite sum is the
**Taylor series** of $f$ at $x_0$. The special case $x_0 = 0$ — by far the
most common — is called the **Maclaurin series**.

<Callout title="The formal statement, with Lagrange remainder" variant="note">
  For a function with $n$ continuous derivatives, the expansion around $x_0$ is
  <em>exact</em> when written with its error term:
  $$
  f(x) = f(x_0) + \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x - x_0)^k + \frac{f^{(n)}(\xi)}{n!}(x - x_0)^n
  $$
  where $\xi$ is some point strictly between $x_0$ and $x$. Reading it in
  three pieces:
  <ul>
    <li>
      <strong>The base value</strong> $f(x_0)$ — the exact anchor of the
      expansion.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>The polynomial sum</strong> — each derivative at $x_0$ scaled by
      the displacement $(x-x_0)^k$ and divided by $k!$.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>The Lagrange remainder</strong> — everything left over, packaged
      as a single $(n+1)$-st-order term. It is handled properly in the next
      section.
    </li>
  </ul>
</Callout>

<KeyIdea>
  <strong>Exactness for polynomials.</strong> If $f$ itself is a polynomial of
  degree $m$, every derivative beyond order $m$ is identically zero. So
  $P_m = f$ exactly, the remainder vanishes, and the Taylor expansion of degree
  $m$ is 100% correct across the whole domain — not just near $x_0$.
</KeyIdea>

$P_n$ collapse; drag $x_0$ and watch the region of good fit move with it.

## The Lagrange remainder

Stopping at degree $n$ is a decision, and it has a cost. The cost has a name
and a formula:

$$
R_n(x) = f(x) - P_n(x) = \frac{f^{(n)}(\xi)}{n!}(x - x_0)^n
$$

for some $\xi$ strictly between $x_0$ and $x$. Three things to absorb:

1. **It is exact, but silent about $\xi$.** The formula is not an inequality —
   the error really equals that expression somewhere in the interval. What you
   do not get is *where*. So in practice you bound $|f^{(n)}(\xi)|$ by its
   largest possible value on the interval and turn the identity into a
   guarantee.

2. **$n!$ is the engine.** Factorials outrun everything: $10! = 3{,}628{,}800$.
   Whatever the numerator does, dividing by $n!$ eventually wins as $n$ grows.

3. **Closeness to $x_0$ is the other lever.** The factor $(x - x_0)^n$ shrinks
   geometrically when $|x - x_0| < 1$, and the two levers multiply. So error
   can be crushed either by taking more terms or by expanding closer to the
   point you evaluate at.

## Popular Taylor series

Six expansions carry most of the weight. The first three are **Maclaurin**
series ($x_0 = 0$) with infinite reach; the last three converge only inside a
window.

| Function      | Taylor series                                                                        | Converges for        |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------- |
| $e^x$         | $$\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{x^k}{k!} = 1 + x + \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^3}{3!} + \cdots$$ | all $x$              |
| $\sin x$      | $$\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} = x - \frac{x^3}{3!} + \frac{x^5}{5!} - \cdots$$ | all $x$              |
| $\cos x$      | $$\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^k x^{2k}}{(2k)!} = 1 - \frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^4}{4!} - \cdots$$ | all $x$              |
| $\ln(1+x)$    | $$\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^{k-1} x^k}{k} = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} - \cdots$$ | $-1 < x \le 1$       |
| $\dfrac{1}{1-x}$ | $$\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} x^k = 1 + x + x^2 + x^3 + \cdots$$              | $|x| < 1$            |
| $(1+x)^\alpha$ | $$\displaystyle 1 + \alpha x + \frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2!}x^2 + \frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)}{3!}x^3 + \cdots$$ | $|x| < 1$            |

A few connections worth noticing:

- $\sin$ and $\cos$ contain only odd and only even powers respectively — the
  signature of an odd and an even function.
- Differentiating $\ln(1+x)$ term by term gives $1 - x + x^2 - \cdots$, which
  is exactly $\frac{1}{1+x}$: the geometric series in disguise. Integrate that
  series and $\ln$ pops back out.
- The binomial series with $\alpha = \tfrac12$ is the expansion of
  $\sqrt{1+x}$ — a pocket square-root calculator.
- The $\ln(1+x)$ series at $x = 1$ becomes the alternating harmonic series,
  which converges — slowly — to $\ln 2$.
