## What continuity means

A function is **continuous** when small changes in the input produce small
changes in the output. Nothing sudden ever happens to it.

Picture yourself walking up a ramp: take a tiny step forward and you rise a
tiny bit. Now picture a staircase: you shuffle forward and stay flat, then at
one instant you jump up a whole step. The ramp is continuous. The staircase is
not — the jump is a break.

Graphically, a continuous function can be drawn in one stroke, without lifting
the pen from the paper. Its graph has:

- no **gaps** (missing points),
- no **jumps** (the graph steps from one height to another), and
- no **asymptotes** (the graph racing off to infinity).

Those three failures are exactly the three ways continuity can break, and each
one gets its own section below. First, let us make "no breaks" precise,
because a picture alone can hide very small breaks.

across the graph and compare the three readouts below it:

- **Continuous** — the three readouts (left limit, right limit, and f(a))
  always agree, so the verdict never changes.
- **Removable hole** — at a = 2 the two sides agree on height 4, but the
  function was defined to be 5, so the dot floats above the hole.
- **Jump** — at a = 2 the left side aims at one height and the right side at
  another; no single value of f(2) can satisfy both.
- **Vertical asymptote** — at a = 3 the function is undefined and the limits
  are infinite.

On the graph, an **open circle** marks a height the curve approaches but does
not take at that input. A **filled circle** marks the value the function
actually takes. The slider's orange guide marks the test input; when it lands
on a break, compare those markers with the left-limit, right-limit, and f(a)
readouts to see exactly which condition fails.

## The formal definition

Here is the definition, stated once in full:

<Callout title="Continuity at a point" variant="note">
  <div>
    A function $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, defined on an open interval
    containing $x=a$, is <strong>continuous at</strong> $a$ if all three
    conditions hold:
  </div>
  <ol>
    <li>
      <strong>The function value exists:</strong> $f(a)$ is a real number.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>The two-sided limit exists:</strong> both one-sided limits
      agree, $\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = L_L$ and $\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L_R$
      with $L_L = L_R = L$.
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>The limit matches the value:</strong>{" "}
      <Equation display={false} tex="\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a)" />.
    </li>
  </ol>
</Callout>

Read the three pieces in plain language:

- $f(a)$ is the height the curve **actually has** at the point $a$.
- $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)$ is the height the curve is **heading toward** as $x$
  approaches $a$ from the left and the right — it says nothing about what
  happens exactly at $a$.
- $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a)$ says where the curve heads and where it lands
  are **the same place**.

Condition 2 carries the left and right limits: both sides must exist and both
must aim at the same height. If they disagree, the limit does not exist — you
have a jump.

<KeyIdea>
  Three questions decide everything: <strong>Is f(a) there? Do both sides
  agree? Does the limit land exactly on f(a)?</strong> If any answer is no,
  the function is discontinuous at that point.
</KeyIdea>

### A worked example

Consider

$$
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2}, & x\neq 2\\[6pt]
5, & x=2
\end{cases}
$$

For every $x \neq 2$ the numerator factors, so

$$
\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}=\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=x+2
$$

The two sides both head for $2+2=4$, so

$$
\lim_{x\to 2}f(x)=4
$$

But the function was defined to be $f(2)=5$. Since $4 \neq 5$, condition 3
fails and the function is **not continuous at 2**. The graph is a straight
line with a hole at height 4 and a stray dot at height 5 — exactly the
removable case you dragged around in the playground.

## The epsilon-delta test

The three-condition test leans on limits. The epsilon-delta test says the
same thing as a game between two people — a skeptic and a defender.

<Callout title="Epsilon-delta definition of continuity" variant="note">
  <div>$f$ is continuous at $a$ if</div>
  <Equation
    tex="\forall\,\varepsilon>0 \;\exists\,\delta>0:\qquad |f(x)-f(a)| < \varepsilon \quad\text{whenever}\quad |x-a| < \delta"
  />
</Callout>

Translating each piece:

- **$\varepsilon$ (epsilon)** is the *challenge*: a tolerance for the output.
  "I will accept your function near $a$ only if the values stay within
  $\varepsilon$ of $f(a)$."
- **$\delta$ (delta)** is your *response*: a window radius around $a$. "Keep
  the input within $\delta$ of $a$, and I promise the output lands inside
  your tolerance."
- **For every $\varepsilon>0$** means the defender must win *every* challenge,
  no matter how tiny.

In symbols: your output error is controlled by your input error,

$$
|x-a|<\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |f(x)-f(a)|<\varepsilon
$$

<Playground id="epsilon-delta" />

Try it yourself:

1. Set a strict tolerance — say $\varepsilon = 0.20$.
2. Drag $\delta$ wide and watch the verdict flip to **fails**: some input
   inside the window climbs outside the band.
3. Shrink $\delta$ (or press **Use a winning δ**) until the verdict reads
   **answers this ε**.
4. Now make $\varepsilon$ tiny and repeat — a winning $\delta$ still exists,
   just smaller.

Why must the definition hold for **every** $\varepsilon>0$? Because gaps hide
in the small scales. If even one tolerance $\varepsilon>0$ had no working
$\delta$, then no matter how close you force $x$ to $a$, some point stays at
least $\varepsilon$ away from $f(a)$ — the curve would visibly miss its own
point, which is precisely a gap or a jump.

## Continuity and differentiability

Two words often get confused:

- **Continuous** — no break; you can draw the graph without lifting the pen.
- **Differentiable** — no break *and* no corner; the graph has a unique
  tangent slope at every point.

The classic example separates them:

$$
f(x)=|x|
$$

At $x=0$ the three conditions all pass: the left side heads for 0, the right
side heads for 0, and $f(0)=0$. The function is continuous there.

Is it differentiable? Coming from the left the graph has slope $-1$; coming
from the right it has slope $+1$. At the corner the slope does not settle on
one value, so the derivative does not exist.

The lesson in one line:

<KeyIdea>
  Differentiable <strong>implies</strong> continuous, but continuous does not
  imply differentiable. Continuity rules out breaks; differentiability rules
  out breaks <em>and</em> corners.
</KeyIdea>

## Three types of discontinuity

When the test at a point fails, the failure has a shape. There are three, and
they map straight onto the three conditions of the definition.

### 1. Removable discontinuity

The limit exists — both sides aim at the same height — but $f(a)$ is either
missing or set to the wrong value.

**Mathematically:** $\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$ exists, but $f(a)\neq L$ (or
$f(a)$ is undefined).

**Example:**

$$
f(x)=\frac{x^2-4}{x-2},\qquad \text{so } f(2)\text{ is undefined},\quad
\lim_{x\to2}f(x)=4
$$

**Why "removable":** one dot repairs it. Redefine $f(2)=4$ and the function
becomes continuous at 2. The break was never structural.

### 2. Jump discontinuity

Both one-sided limits exist and are finite, but they are not equal.

**Mathematically:** $\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=L_L$ and
$\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=L_R$ with $L_L \neq L_R$.

**Example:**

$$
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
2, & x<3\\
6, & x\geq 3
\end{cases}
\qquad L_L=2,\quad L_R=6
$$

**Why not removable:** no single value of $f(3)$ can equal both 2 and 6.
Redefining the point only moves the dot — the step stays.

### 3. Infinite discontinuity

The function grows without bound near $a$; the graph has a vertical
asymptote.

**Mathematically:** at least one one-sided limit is $\pm\infty$ (so no finite
limit exists, and $f(a)$ cannot be a real number).

**Example:**

$$
f(x)=\frac{1}{x-2}
\qquad \lim_{x\to2^-}f(x)=-\infty,\qquad
\lim_{x\to2^+}f(x)=+\infty
$$

**Why not removable:** you cannot fill the hole with any real number — the
graph never settles near one.

## Continuity on an interval

At an interior point, the usual three-condition test applies. The endpoints of
a closed interval need special care, because beyond an endpoint there is
nothing to approach from.

<Callout title="Continuity on [a, b]" variant="note">
  <div>
    $f$ is <strong>continuous on the closed interval</strong> $[a,b]$ when it
    satisfies:
  </div>
  <ol>
    <li>the ordinary continuity test at every point strictly inside, $a < x < b$;</li>
    <li>
      <strong>continuity from the right at</strong> $a$:{" "}
      <Equation display={false} tex="\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=f(a)" />
    </li>
    <li>
      <strong>continuity from the left at</strong> $b$:{" "}
      <Equation display={false} tex="\lim_{x\to b^-}f(x)=f(b)" />
    </li>
  </ol>
</Callout>

Why one-sided at the ends? The two-sided limit at $a$ would require
approaching $a$ from the left, but no points exist to the left of $a$ — the
domain stops there. The same holds at $b$ from the right. The one-sided
limits are the only ones the domain can support.

For example, $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,1]$ is continuous: at every $x>0$ the
ordinary test passes, and at the left endpoint $x=0$ only the right side
exists, with $\lim_{x\to0^+}\sqrt{x}=0=f(0)$.

## Algebra of continuous functions

Continuity survives the usual algebraic operations. Suppose $f$ and $g$ are
continuous at $a$ and $c$ is a constant. Then all of these are continuous at
$a$:

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Combination</th><th>Condition</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>f(x) + g(x) and f(x) − g(x)</td><td>None beyond continuity of f and g</td></tr>
    <tr><td>c · f(x)</td><td>None</td></tr>
    <tr><td>f(x) · g(x)</td><td>None</td></tr>
    <tr><td>f(x) / g(x)</td><td>Requires g(a) ≠ 0</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

The quotient is the only one with a catch: if $g(a)=0$ the denominator
vanishes and the function is undefined there — the graph may blow up.

Two useful consequences:

- **Polynomials are continuous everywhere.** They are built from $x$ and
  constants using only addition and multiplication, so the rules above apply
  at every point. For example $f(x)=x^2+3x-2$ passes the test at every $x$,
  including $x=2$.
- **Rational functions are continuous wherever the denominator is not
  zero.** For $f(x)=\dfrac{x+1}{x-3}$ every point is fine except $x=3$, where
  the denominator dies and the function is not even defined — so the domain
  of a rational function already excludes its problem points.

## The Mean Value Theorem

<Callout title="Mean Value Theorem (MVT)" variant="note">
  <div>
    If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, there
    exists at least one $c \in (a, b)$ such that
  </div>
  <Equation tex="f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}" />
</Callout>

## Continuity in economics

Economic models lean on continuity constantly, usually without saying so.
When we write a demand curve such as

$$
Q_d = 100 - 3P
$$

we are assuming price and quantity can move by arbitrarily small amounts and
the quantity demanded moves by a correspondingly small amount. That is exactly
continuity.

Is the real world continuous? Strictly, no — you buy apples in whole apples,
and the smallest price move is one penny. But modelling is a choice: if
counting in pennies instead of dollars gives the same insight, the
granularity does not matter. What matters is whether assuming smooth, small
changes improves the model or hides something. Continuity buys us powerful
tools — approximations and derivatives — and those tools assume changes stay
small.

### A continuous cost function

Suppose a firm's total cost is

$$
C(q)=100+5q
$$

where $q$ is output. This is a polynomial, so it is continuous everywhere.

- $C(10)=100+5(10)=150$.
- Continuity at $q=10$ means: produce slightly more or slightly less than 10
  and the cost changes only slightly. If output moves by $\Delta q$, cost
  moves by $\Delta C=5\,\Delta q$ — proportional, never a jump.
- Continuity does **not** require linearity. $C(q)=q^2+100$ and
  $C(q)=\sqrt{q}+100$ are continuous too. Any polynomial, any rational
  function away from division by zero, all pass.

### A discontinuous pricing policy

Now a firm that charges

$$
P(q)=
\begin{cases}
100q, & q<10\\
100q-200, & q\geq10
\end{cases}
$$

Approaching 10 units from below, the bill heads for $1000$. At exactly 10
units the firm charges $800$. The left-hand limit is 1000, the right-hand
value is 800 — a **jump discontinuity** with a 200-unit discount hanging on
the threshold.

Economically this reads as a bulk-discount cliff: the last step from 9.99 to
10.00 units *reduces* the bill. Firms really do price this way, but the jump
breaks the smooth-trade-off assumption — a tiny change in quantity can change
expenditure by a lot, and the calculus of optimisation no longer applies
across the threshold.

And note the limit of continuity's promise: continuity alone does not locate
the best output. Finding it needs more — derivatives, or simply checking
values.

## Continuity in several variables

Everything so far used inputs on a line. For a function

$$
f:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}
$$

the input is a *point* $\mathbf{x}=(x,y)$, and distances are measured with
the Euclidean metric — straight-line distance:

$$
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}\|
=\sqrt{(x-a_1)^2+(y-a_2)^2}
$$

The definition then reads:

$$
\forall\,\varepsilon>0\;\exists\,\delta>0:
\qquad |f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{a})|<\varepsilon
\quad\text{whenever}\quad
\sqrt{(x-a_1)^2+(y-a_2)^2}<\delta
$$

The set of inputs within $\delta$ of $\mathbf{a}$ is the **open
$\delta$-ball** (in the plane, a disk):

$$
B_\delta(\mathbf{a})
=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n : \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{a}\|<\delta\}
$$

So the picture is unchanged, only one dimension richer: *every input inside
the $\delta$-disk must land inside the $\varepsilon$-band around
$f(\mathbf{a})$.*

unchanged.
